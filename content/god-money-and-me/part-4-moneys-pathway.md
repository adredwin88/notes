# God, Money, and Me — Part 4
## Money's Pathway

> [!info] Curriculum
> **Series:** God, Money, and Me
> **Part:** 4 of 4
> **Main theme:** The four components that, in Paul's framework, work together to build a financially freer future
> **Key concept:** Stewarding, seeding, saving, and spending
> **Key question:** **How can I give every part of my financial increase a purpose and build a legacy for future generations?**

---

# 1. Opening — Bringing the Curriculum Together

Part 1 explored **Money's Myths**.

Part 2 explored **Money's Spirit**, including Paul's teaching about Mammon and the influence money can have over our hearts and decisions.

Part 3 explored **Money's Test**: the way we respond to financial increase can reveal which authority we are honoring.

Part 4 brings those themes together.

Paul calls this final session:

> **Money's Pathway.**

His central point is that a financially freer future is not usually built through one isolated financial habit. In his framework, four components need to work together:

1. **Stewarding**
2. **Seeding**
3. **Saving**
4. **Spending**

Paul says that he and Marie had practiced some of these components for years, but he had not understood the importance of saving. Understanding all four components changed how he viewed the pathway to financial freedom.

The central question is no longer only:

> "How much money do I give?"

It becomes:

> **"How do I intentionally manage everything that comes into my hands?"**

---

# 2. The Central Scripture — 2 Corinthians 9:8

Paul repeatedly returns to 2 Corinthians 9:8 as the central Scripture for the whole curriculum.

The NLT expresses the verse this way:

> "And God will generously provide all you need. Then you will always have everything you need and plenty left over to share with others."

Paul emphasizes three ideas in this passage:

- God is able to provide grace.
- Believers can experience sufficiency.
- That sufficiency can enable them to do good works and share with others.

Paul pays particular attention to the words **sufficiency** and **abundance**. He describes sufficiency as a state in which a person is not continually dependent on extra aid or support to meet life's needs.

In his framework, financial capacity is not meant to end with personal comfort. It should also create the ability to contribute to good works and bless others.

The verse therefore shapes the direction of the pathway:

```text
God's provision and grace
        ↓
Sufficiency
        ↓
Capacity for good works
        ↓
Blessing beyond ourselves
```

**Important distinction:** Paul applies this passage to a vision of financial freedom. The verse itself speaks about God's grace, sufficiency, and generosity; Christians may differ about how directly it promises material abundance or financial independence. It should not be treated as a guarantee of wealth or as a reason to blame people who experience financial hardship.

---

# 3. The Goal — More Than Having Money

Paul is not presenting financial freedom as simply becoming wealthy or being able to buy whatever we want.

He describes a future in which people can meet their responsibilities, have greater financial stability, contribute to good works, and create a better starting point for future generations.

In this framework, money becomes a resource that can serve a larger purpose.

The goal is not merely:

> "I have enough money for myself."

The broader vision is:

> **"I can manage what I have, meet my responsibilities, help others, and leave a stronger foundation for those who come after me."**

Paul encourages people to think generationally. Today's decisions can affect children, grandchildren, and people beyond our own lifetime.

---

# 4. Why Four Components Are Needed

Paul explains that he and Marie had already been honoring God with the first tenth, giving generously, and being frugal in their spending.

But he had not understood the importance of saving.

He uses two illustrations to explain why all the components matter.

## The concrete illustration

Concrete needs the right ingredients working together, including cement, sand, water, and stone. Having only some of the necessary ingredients does not produce the same result.

## The cooking illustration

Paul also recalls his years as a pastry cook. He says that substituting a convenient ingredient for the ingredient a recipe actually requires can change the result. The ingredients need to be right and balanced.

He applies both illustrations to personal finances:

> **The components need to work together in the right balance.**

A person may be generous but have no savings. Another may save diligently but never consider generosity or the purpose of money. Someone else may focus only on spending.

Paul's framework calls for a more complete structure.

---

# 5. The Four Components at a Glance

Paul identifies four components:

```text
1. STEWARDING
   Recognize and honor God's portion

2. SEEDING
   Plant seed with the expectation of a harvest

3. SAVING
   Build equity and prepare for future generations

4. SPENDING
   Live within intentional boundaries
```

Each component has a distinct purpose.

| Component | Purpose in Paul's framework |
|---|---|
| Stewarding | Honor what Paul identifies as God's portion |
| Seeding | Release seed and participate in the principle of sowing and reaping |
| Saving | Build future capacity and generational legacy |
| Spending | Meet everyday needs while living within predetermined limits |

Paul's point is that one component should not be expected to replace the others.

---

# 6. Component One — Stewarding

Part 3 introduced **stewarding** as the first component.

Paul's starting point is that the first tenth belongs to God. He presents tithing as an act of returning and honoring what belongs to God, rather than giving away something that is entirely ours.

In his framework, stewardship begins by recognizing God's authority over our financial lives.

The question is not only:

> "Am I generous?"

It is also:

> **"Do I intentionally honor what I believe belongs to God before deciding how to use the rest?"**

Paul connects this teaching with Malachi 3 and its references to returning to God, tithes, offerings, and the storehouse.

He also discusses practical questions about tithing, including whether it is calculated before or after tax and what counts as financial increase.

These are part of Paul's teaching framework. The passage and the questions around Christian tithing should be considered in their biblical and theological context.

---

# 7. Tithing, Tax, and Disposable Income

Paul acknowledges that people ask whether they should tithe before or after tax.

His reasoning is that people who calculate their tithe only from net income may be overlooking the benefits they receive from the taxes paid in the country where they live.

He also asks what should count as a person's **increase**. His position is that when a person expands or advances financially or materially, the first tenth should be used to honor God.

Paul then distinguishes between:

- the portion he identifies as God's;
- tax paid to the government;
- disposable income that remains available for the other parts of the pathway.

His teaching is that the first portion belongs to God and should not be treated as interchangeable with the other portions.

**Practical note:** The transcript's wording moves between the first tenth of increase and the allocation of disposable income. When applying the model, make sure the calculation's base is explicit. Do not accidentally count the same money twice or assume that every percentage mentioned uses the same denominator.

---

# 8. Component Two — Seeding

The second component is **seeding**.

Paul describes a seed as carrying the potential for a harvest greater than itself. He connects the idea to the biblical principle of seedtime and harvest.

He refers to Genesis 8:22:

> **"While the earth remains, seedtime and harvest ... shall not cease."**

Paul's emphasis is that seedtime and harvest are recurring features of life on earth.

He then refers to Galatians 6:7:

> **"Whatever a man or woman sows, they will also reap."**

Paul uses a farmer as an illustration. A farmer does not plant one kind of seed and expect a completely unrelated crop.

```text
Seed
  ↓
Harvest
```

Paul applies this principle to financial giving and says that financial breakthrough and financial blessing require financial seed.

---

# 9. What Paul Means by Financial Seeding

Paul connects seeding with the idea of releasing something now that can contribute to a future harvest.

In his framework, seeding is not simply an extra expense. It is a deliberate part of the financial pathway.

He also connects seeding with generosity: the seed can be given where a person feels led, helping other people while trusting God with the outcome.

Paul's teaching can be summarized this way:

> **Stewarding honors God's portion; seeding releases a portion with the expectation of a harvest.**

He says, "The more the seed, the greater the harvest."

That statement belongs to Paul's interpretation of the sowing-and-reaping principle. It should not be turned into a mechanical guarantee that a particular monetary gift will produce a specific financial return. The biblical principle of sowing and reaping has a wider context than a formula for becoming wealthy.

---

# 10. Stewarding and Seeding Work Together

Paul explicitly says that stewarding and seeding go together.

In his model, the first portion is honored as God's, while another portion is intentionally set aside as seed.

```text
Financial increase
        ↓
Stewarding
        ↓
Honor God's portion
        ↓
Seeding
        ↓
Release seed
        ↓
Potential harvest
```

The two components have different purposes. Paul does not treat generosity as a replacement for stewardship, nor does he treat stewardship as a reason to stop being generous.

The practical change is to decide in advance what each portion is for.

---

# 11. Component Three — Saving

The third component is **saving**.

Paul says this was the component he had not previously understood. He and Marie had been honoring God, giving generously, and living frugally, but they had not fully grasped the role of saving.

This became a significant realization for him.

Saving is not simply what happens if money happens to remain at the end of the month. In Paul's framework, saving is an intentional allocation with a future purpose.

He connects saving to building equity and creating a financial foundation that can outlast the present generation.

---

# 12. Saving Creates a Generational Echo

Paul refers to Proverbs 13:22:

> **"A good person leaves an inheritance for their children's children."**

He uses this verse to encourage a generational way of thinking.

Legacy, in his description, is not only about living well in the present. It is also about making decisions today that can create a better starting point for the next generation.

```text
What I build today
        ↓
What I leave behind
        ↓
My children
        ↓
My children's children
```

Paul gives the example of equity in a home as something that can have a generational effect.

The core idea is:

> **Saving can build something that continues to benefit people after our own lifetime.**

That does not mean every financial decision will automatically produce an inheritance. It means Paul wants saving to be treated as a purposeful component of long-term planning.

---

# 13. Saving Is Not the Same as Spending Later

Paul distinguishes saving from spending.

In his framework, saving is intended to build equity and increase net worth rather than simply accumulating money for future consumption.

He says that if someone is in debt, the first use of the saving portion may be to reduce that debt. Once debt is addressed, he presents a home that appreciates in value as one possible way to build an asset that can benefit future generations.

This is Paul's suggested pathway, not a universal financial rule for every person or situation. Debt, housing, liquidity, risk, and local financial conditions require individual judgment.

The underlying principle is to give the saving portion a purpose beyond immediate consumption.

---

# 14. The Problem of Instant Gratification

Paul identifies instant gratification as one of the major challenges people face.

The surrounding culture often encourages the thought:

> "I want everything now."

Paul asks people to consider a different question:

> **"What can I build now that will still make a difference later?"**

He believes that a generational perspective requires restraint in the present.

Instead of using every available resource for immediate wants, people can choose to build equity and capacity for the future.

This is not a rejection of enjoying life. It is a challenge to avoid allowing immediate desires to consume every resource that could serve a longer-term purpose.

---

# 15. The 10-10-80 Principle

Paul calls the model he teaches the **10-10-80 principle**.

He describes the first tenth as God's portion, then discusses tax and the disposable income that remains. From disposable income, he encourages people to work toward allocating:

- 10% to seeding;
- 10% to saving;
- 80% to spending.

In simplified form, the allocation of disposable income is:

```text
Disposable income
        ↓
10% — Seeding
        ↓
10% — Saving
        ↓
80% — Spending
```

This sits alongside Paul's teaching that the first tenth belongs to God and tax is paid to the government.

**Important calculation distinction:** The name "10-10-80" can be confusing if the first tenth, tax, and disposable-income percentages are all treated as if they use one identical base. In the transcript, Paul describes the first tenth of increase, tax, and then the 10% seeding, 10% saving, and 80% spending allocations from disposable income. Keep these stages distinct when doing the arithmetic.

Paul also presents this as a principle to work toward, not necessarily a pattern everyone can implement immediately.

---

# 16. Four Containers or Four Accounts

Paul says that his church encourages people to think in terms of four containers or four accounts.

When financial increase arrives, the portions are assigned to different purposes rather than being treated as one undifferentiated pool.

```text
Financial increase
        ↓
┌─────────────────────┐
│ God's portion       │
├─────────────────────┤
│ Seeding             │
├─────────────────────┤
│ Saving              │
├─────────────────────┤
│ Spending            │
└─────────────────────┘
```

The containers do not have to be four literal bank accounts. The point is that the purposes are separated clearly enough that spending money does not quietly consume money assigned to another purpose.

The key principle is:

> **Decide the purpose of the money before the money is spent.**

---

# 17. A Story About Learning to Save Young

Paul describes a nephew who began applying the four-component approach at a young age.

The nephew set aside the first tenth for God, another tenth as seed, and another tenth for saving. He understood the saving portion as a way to build toward a future home and create a financial foundation for later generations.

Years later, he told Paul that he had accumulated several thousand dollars in his saving account.

Paul uses the story to show how early habits can shape a person's relationship with money over time.

The point is not that everyone will accumulate the same amount or achieve the same outcome. The example illustrates the value of learning to assign money a purpose before spending it.

---

# 18. A Practical Starting Point — Begin Where You Are

Paul acknowledges that some people may feel unable to live on the spending portion of the model right now.

His advice is not to give up simply because the complete allocation feels impossible.

Instead, he encourages people to begin at a realistic starting point and take a step forward.

He describes the 10-10-80 principle as a goal people can work toward. For someone who cannot begin with the full allocation, he suggests starting with a smaller seeding percentage and building from there.

The principle is:

> **Start where you are, but keep moving intentionally toward a healthier structure.**

This approach recognizes that people have different incomes, obligations, debt levels, and living costs.

---

# 19. Moving from 10-10-80 to 10-20-60

Paul says that the 10-10-80 principle is not necessarily the final destination.

As financial capacity increases, he describes moving toward **10-20-60**:

- the first tenth remains God's portion in his framework;
- tax is paid;
- 20% of disposable income is allocated to seeding;
- 20% of disposable income is allocated to saving;
- 60% of disposable income is used for spending.

The simplified allocation of disposable income becomes:

```text
Disposable income
        ↓
20% — Seeding
        ↓
20% — Saving
        ↓
60% — Spending
```

Paul's emphasis is on increasing the portions devoted to seed and future generations as capacity grows, rather than automatically allowing spending to grow at the same rate as income.

As with 10-10-80, the calculation base matters: the percentages shown here refer to the disposable-income allocation described in the transcript, after the earlier stages of the model.

---

# 20. Component Four — Spending

The fourth component is **spending**.

Paul recognizes that spending is necessary. It is the portion through which people live and meet their everyday needs.

His concern is that spending can become excessive when people assume they need more than they actually do.

He says that meaningful futures require people to live within their means.

The purpose of the spending portion is not to create guilt about every purchase. It is to make sure everyday spending operates within boundaries that protect the other components.

```text
Income
  ↓
Intentional allocation
  ↓
Spending within boundaries
  ↓
Less financial pressure
  ↓
Greater everyday peace
```

In Paul's framework, living within one's means can reduce the worry that comes from continually spending more than is available.

---

# 21. Predetermine Spending Boundaries

Paul's first practical challenge about spending is to **live within predetermined boundaries**.

Rather than waiting until a purchase opportunity appears and deciding in the moment whether to buy it, he encourages people to decide their limits beforehand.

The sequence changes from:

```text
See something I want
        ↓
Decide whether to buy it
        ↓
Find a way to pay
```

to:

```text
Set financial boundaries
        ↓
Understand the spending portion
        ↓
Make purchases within those limits
```

Predetermined limits help prevent each new desire from becoming a fresh negotiation with the budget.

The practical question becomes:

> **"Does this purchase fit the purpose and limit I already decided on?"**

---

# 22. Delayed Gratification

Paul describes this as the principle of **delayed gratification**.

Delayed gratification means accepting restraint today for the sake of a future outcome.

In this teaching, it means limiting spending to the portion one has intentionally chosen, whether that is 80%, 90%, 96%, or another realistic amount while working toward the broader model.

Paul's emphasis is that restraint today can create more room for tomorrow.

The choice is not always between buying something and never buying it. Sometimes it is between buying it immediately and waiting until it fits the plan.

> **There is restraint needed today for tomorrow's breakthrough.**

That is Paul's practical challenge: do not let immediate desire automatically overrule a considered financial decision.

---

# 23. "I Couldn't Live on That Percentage"

Paul anticipates the objection that a person could not currently live on the proposed spending percentage.

His response is to consider a second path:

> **Discover a way to increase your income.**

He challenges people not to assume that what they are doing now is all they can ever do.

This does not mean that every person can increase income immediately, or that financial hardship is always caused by a lack of effort. Paul's point is to consider whether there is productive potential that has not yet been explored.

He encourages people to begin with a realistic step rather than abandon the pathway altogether.

---

# 24. Increase Income and Use Your Potential

Paul says:

> **"Never underestimate your God-given and latent potential."**

He gives an example from his own life. While working on a church building project in Sydney, he made coffee tables after his regular work hours and sold them at weekend markets.

He is not saying that everyone should make and sell coffee tables.

His broader point is that there may be productive opportunities around us that we have not considered.

Examples he mentions include:

- making something that can be sold;
- taking a second job for a season;
- studying or qualifying for a better job;
- using time that would otherwise be unproductive.

These examples are invitations to examine possible opportunities, not a guarantee that every side project will succeed.

---

# 25. More Income Should Create More Room, Not Just More Spending

Paul connects increased income to the financial structure.

If income increases but spending expands automatically, the additional income may not create the future capacity he is describing.

His model encourages people to keep the allocation intentional as income grows.

```text
Income increases
        ↓
Keep the financial structure
        ↓
More capacity for seeding and saving
        ↓
Greater room for future needs and goals
```

The goal is not simply:

> "Earn more so I can spend more."

It is:

> **"If I earn more, how can I use the increase to build greater capacity?"**

---

# 26. Don't Spend What You Don't Have

Paul explicitly warns:

> **"Don't spend what you don't have."**

He connects this principle to his concerns about destructive debt.

Debt can become a problem when it takes away future choices or begins to determine major decisions.

The question is not only whether a monthly payment looks affordable today.

It is also:

> **"What will this commitment do to my future freedom and financial capacity?"**

Paul's concern is that spending decisions made now can place limits on future choices.

---

# 27. Destructive Debt

Paul specifically warns against debt used to purchase depreciating consumer assets, giving examples such as a new phone, car, television, refrigerator, and other consumer goods.

His concern is that the asset may lose value while the person continues paying the debt.

He presents this as one reason people can remain under financial pressure.

This is Paul's financial position, and individual debt decisions still require attention to the terms, purpose, affordability, and circumstances involved. Not every form of borrowing is identical.

The practical warning is to be especially careful when borrowing to support a lifestyle or acquire items that lose value.

---

# 28. The Practical Test for Debt

Paul gives a test that connects debt with the larger theme of authority.

If debt begins to determine what we can do, where we can go, what opportunities we can take, or which decisions we can make, it has moved beyond a simple payment obligation.

In Paul's framework, debt can begin to occupy a place of authority when it controls important choices.

The question becomes:

> **"Is this debt restricting my ability to make future decisions according to my values and responsibilities?"**

This is a deeper question than whether a lender will approve the application or whether the monthly payment can be squeezed into the budget.

# 30. Financial Accountability

Paul identifies another practical step:

> **Activate accountability partners.**

He says that he believes good decisions are managed decisions. In other areas of life, people often accept coaching, mentoring, and feedback. He asks why people might not apply the same approach to their finances.

Paul connects this idea with Proverbs 11:14, which emphasizes the value of counsel.

The point is not to give another person ownership of our money. It is to make room for honest questions, correction, and support.

A trusted accountability partner may help someone notice patterns they are overlooking and follow through on the decisions they have already chosen to make.

---

# 31. Why Accountability Matters

Paul says that financial accountability can connect a person's desire to the decision needed to move toward it.

For example:

```text
Desire
  ↓
Decision
  ↓
Action
  ↓
Review and accountability
```

A person may sincerely want financial freedom but continue making choices that work against that goal.

Accountability creates a place to ask:

- What decision do I need to make?
- Am I following the plan I set?
- Is a habit undermining my progress?
- Do I need advice or a different approach?

Paul also frames accountability as a way to bring financial decisions out of secrecy and into the light, rather than allowing harmful patterns to remain unexamined.

---

# 32. Choosing an Accountability Partner

The practical application is to identify trustworthy people who can speak honestly about financial decisions.

A helpful accountability partner should not simply shame someone or take control. The relationship should support honest reflection, responsible decisions, and follow-through.

The person may be a mature Christian, a mentor, or someone with relevant experience and sound judgment.

Paul's emphasis is on having someone who can help connect:

> **desire → decision → action**

This is particularly important when the desired change requires consistent choices over time rather than one dramatic decision.

---

---

### ❌ Instead of:

> "I know what I am doing, so I do not need advice."

### ✅ Do this:

# 34. The Four Components Must Work Together

Paul returns to the idea that the four components need to work together.

```text
STEWARDING
Honor God's portion
        ↓
SEEDING
Release seed
        ↓
SAVING
Build future capacity
        ↓
SPENDING
Live within intentional boundaries
```

These components have different functions.

- Stewarding concerns what Paul identifies as God's portion.
- Seeding concerns what is released as seed.
- Saving concerns future capacity and generational legacy.
- Spending concerns everyday life within boundaries.

The point is not to choose one component and ignore the others. Paul wants people to build a balanced structure in which each portion has a purpose.

# 35. The Bigger Picture — A Financial Future Is Built

Paul's overall pathway can be summarized as:

```text
Financial increase
        ↓
Stewarding
        ↓
Seeding
        ↓
Saving
        ↓
Spending within boundaries
        ↓
Greater financial capacity
        ↓
More opportunity for good works
        ↓

```

This is not simply a strategy for getting more money.

It is a way of giving each portion of financial increase a defined purpose.

Paul's vision is that financial freedom should create more than personal comfort. It should make room for responsibility, generosity, good works, and a legacy that can benefit others.

---

# 36. What Paul Is Really Saying

Paul's deeper message is that financial freedom does not come from one habit alone.

Being generous matters in his framework, but generosity is not the whole system.

Earning more can create additional room, but more income without intentional boundaries may simply lead to more spending.

Saving can build future capacity, but Paul wants it to operate alongside stewardship, seeding, and spending.

Spending is necessary, but without boundaries it can consume resources intended for other purposes.

Therefore:

> **The ingredients have to work together.**

His practical challenge is to stop treating money as one large pool and start intentionally assigning it to different purposes.

---

# 37. The Most Important Practical Shift

The shift is from:

```text
"I receive money.
Then I decide what to do with it."
```

to:

```text
"I receive money.
Then I intentionally assign each portion
according to its purpose."
```

The first approach can become reactive: money arrives, needs and wants compete for it, and whatever remains may be assigned to long-term goals.

The second approach is intentional: the purposes are decided before spending begins.

The practical change is to create a plan for the money before the money is absorbed by everyday decisions.

# 39. Personal Reflection — Which Component Needs Attention?

Part 4 invites us to examine not only whether we handle money well in one area, but whether the components work together.

Ask yourself:

- **Stewarding:** Have I thought intentionally about what I believe belongs to God and how I will honor that?
- **Seeding:** Do I plan for generosity and giving, or do I leave it entirely to whatever remains?
- **Saving:** Am I building any financial capacity for future needs and future generations?
- **Spending:** Have I set boundaries before making purchases?
- **Income:** Are there realistic opportunities to develop skills or increase income?
- **Debt:** Are my financial commitments limiting future choices?
- **Accountability:** Is there a trustworthy person who can help me evaluate and follow through on financial decisions?
- **Generations:** What might my current habits make possible—or more difficult—for those who come after me?

These questions are not a way to measure personal worth. They are prompts for honest reflection and practical change.

# 41. Important Distinction — Paul's Teaching vs. Explicit Biblical Statements

> [!warning] Read this distinction carefully
> Some conclusions in this curriculum are **Paul's theological and practical interpretation** of the Scriptures he cites. They should not automatically be treated as the only possible interpretation of those passages.

In Part 4, Paul makes interpretive claims about:

- financial freedom as a pathway that can be developed;
- the first tenth as God's portion;
- financial seeding as a way of participating in harvest;
- saving as a way to create a generational echo;
- the 10-10-80 and 10-20-60 models;
- debt that restricts choices as potentially destructive;
- financial accountability as a way to support better decisions;
- the four components working together to create greater financial capacity.

These belong to **Paul's framework**.

The passages he cites—including 2 Corinthians 9:8, Genesis 8:22, Galatians 6:7, Galatians 6:9, Proverbs 13:22, and Proverbs 11:14—should still be read in their own literary and historical contexts.

The financial percentages are a practical model Paul recommends, not a biblical command stated in those exact percentages. Likewise, sowing and reaping should not be reduced to a guarantee that a particular monetary gift will generate a specific financial return.

# 42. The Curriculum in One View

The four parts build on one another:

```text
PART 1 — MONEY'S MYTHS
Examine beliefs about money
        ↓
PART 2 — MONEY'S SPIRIT
Examine money's influence on the heart
        ↓
PART 3 — MONEY'S TEST
Examine the response to financial increase
        ↓
PART 4 — MONEY'S PATHWAY
Give every portion a purpose
```

Part 4 brings the earlier themes into practical financial choices.

The question is no longer only what we believe about money or whether money has become an authority. It also becomes how we manage what comes into our hands.

# 43. Part 4 — In One Sentence

> **Money's Pathway is Paul's framework for intentionally managing financial increase through stewarding, seeding, saving, and spending so that today's decisions can create greater capacity and benefit future generations.**

---

# 44. Closing Scripture — Keep Doing Good

Paul closes by returning to the biblical call to persevere in doing good.

He refers to Galatians 6:9–10, emphasizing that people should not grow weary in doing good because, in due season, they will reap if they do not give up. He also highlights the opportunity to do good to everyone, especially those in the household of faith.

He then returns to 2 Corinthians 9:8 and the conviction that God is able to make grace abound so that believers have sufficiency and an abundance for every good work.

Paul encourages people to discuss the four components with others and consider what could happen if stewarding, seeding, saving, and spending came into alignment.

The final emphasis is not simply to memorize the percentages. It is to seek understanding, challenge the roadblocks in our thinking, and take practical steps toward a more intentional financial life.

# 45. Final Reflection

Before moving on, consider these questions:

1. Which of the four components have I practiced consistently?
2. Which component have I neglected or misunderstood?
3. What is one realistic change I can make with my next financial increase?
4. What boundary could help me avoid spending reactively?
5. What could I do to build future capacity for my family or others?
6. Who could help me reflect honestly and remain accountable?
7. How could greater financial capacity enable me to do good works?

> **The practical invitation:** Begin where you are, give each portion a purpose, and keep taking intentional steps forward.
